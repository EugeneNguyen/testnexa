# 21 — Competition Test

**Note on numbering:** requested as "19 — Competition Test," renumbered to 21. See [00-overview.md](00-overview.md) for the full index.

## Test applied

For each of the 20 concepts (15): does it introduce a factor the test-management industry has never competed on at all — reaching a buyer/behavior/budget genuinely outside the industry's current competitive frame (**NEW MARKET SPACE**) — or does it make the offering more attractive to a buyer who is already being fought over on an already-competed factor (**COMPETE BETTER, EXISTING MARKET**)? This is a stricter, more skeptical test than 18's noncustomer-tier mapping or 19's scoring — a concept can convert a noncustomer (18) or score well (19) and still fail this test if it does so by improving an existing, already-contested factor rather than introducing an uncontested one. Cross-checked against each concept's ERRC classification (15) as a consistency signal, not as the sole answer.

## Result after elimination

**Eliminated (13 concepts, mainly competitive improvements):** 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 15, 16, 17, 18, 20.

**Survives as genuine new-market-space concepts (6):** **1, 2, 10, 12, 14, 19.**

**Held out separately (1, not a value-curve concept):** 13 (auditor/consultant partner program) — a distribution mechanism to attach to whichever of the six survivors gets built, not a competing product idea in its own right.

## Insight

**INSIGHT:** This test produces a materially different shortlist than 19's raw scoring, which is the point of running it as a separate, stricter filter. **#5 (continuous traceability) was in 19's top 5 by score but fails this test** — it's a high-value, hard-to-copy improvement to an existing factor, not a new market. **#10 (policy-as-code) and #12 (ISTQB sandbox) survive this test despite ranking outside 19's top 5** — they open uncontested territory even though their raw customer-value/market-potential scores were more modest. **RECOMMENDATION:** the eliminated 13 aren't worthless — several (especially #4, #6, #18, #20) are legitimate table-stakes work needed to be competitive at all — but per this discovery process's own definition of Blue Ocean strategy (the goal is uncontested space, not a better version of an existing product), they should be scheduled as supporting/retention work, not marketed as the reason a customer switches. The six survivors — **1, 2, 10, 12, 14, 19** — are the actual candidate set for a value-innovation-grade MVP; 20's fit analysis already surfaced real weaknesses in four of the five it examined (14, 19, 1, 5), so "survives this test" is necessary but not sufficient — willingness to pay (22) is the next filter.
